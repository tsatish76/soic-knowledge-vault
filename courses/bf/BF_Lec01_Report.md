## BF-Lec01 – Stage 3: Working Memory & Cognitive Reflection Traps [00:06:06–00:13:39]

### a) What this stage answers

Why does System 1 consistently fire first and dominate intuitive math, working memory, and visual judgment, and why is this a universal feature of human psychology rather than a personal flaw?

### b) Detailed explanation

#### Intuitive vs. Analytical Math (Cognitive Reflection)

The speaker presents the classic bat-and-ball puzzle: the total cost of a bat and a ball is **₹110**, and the bat costs **₹100 more** than the ball—how much does the ball cost? [00:06:06–00:07:17] [Slide 5]. Roughly **50%–60%** of participants immediately answer **₹10** (because $110 - 100 = 10$ feels effortless to System 1\) [00:07:17–00:08:02]. However, if the ball cost ₹10, the bat would cost ₹110, making the total ₹120 [00:07:17, 00:08:59]. Engaging System 2 shows the ball costs **₹5** and the bat costs **₹105** ($105 + 5 = 110$, and $105 - 5 = 100$) [00:08:02–00:08:59].

#### Working Memory vs. Deliberate Effort

Comparing `2 × 2` against `17 × 26`, the speaker explains why `2 × 2 = 4` comes out instantaneously: it sits inside **working memory** as a memorized fact [00:09:47–00:10:23] [Slide 5–6]. By contrast, `17 × 26` is not stored in working memory, forcing the brain to pause, allocate effort, and compute analytically via System 2 [00:09:47–00:10:23] [Slide 6].

#### Visual Perception Illusion & System 1 Dominance

Next, the speaker shows two horizontal lines and asks which line is shorter, giving participants 10–20 seconds to slow down [00:11:06–00:11:52] [Slide 7]. Responses split between "first is shorter" and "second is shorter," yet **both lines are identical in length** [00:11:52–00:13:09] [Slide 7]. Summarizing all three experiments (triangles, bat-and-ball, horizontal lines), the speaker reassures learners that getting these wrong is **not a personal fault**: System 1 **always fires first and is usually more dominant** because of how human psychology is wired [00:13:09–00:13:39] [Slide 8].

### c) Examples & case studies

- **Example 1: Bat-and-Ball Problem** [00:06:06–00:08:59] [Slide 5]  
    
  - **Context:** Testing intuitive vs. analytical arithmetic under a 20-second timer.  
  - **Data/details:** Bat \+ Ball \= **₹110**; Bat \= Ball \+ **₹100**. **50%–60%** guessed **₹10**; correct answer \= **₹5** (Bat \= ₹105).  
  - **Walkthrough:** System 1 subtracts 100 from 110 to get 10, ignoring that the difference between 100 and 10 is only 90, not 100\.  
  - **Takeaway:** Intuitive answers feel right immediately but fail when constraints require multi-step verification.


- **Example 2: `2 × 2` vs. `17 × 26` Working Memory Test** [00:09:47–00:10:23] [Slide 5–6]

  - **Walkthrough:** Facts in working memory trigger instant answers; unfamiliar problems require conscious mental work.  
  - **Takeaway:** Real investing decisions resemble `17 × 26`—they cannot be solved by reflex.


- **Example 3: Horizontal Lines Visual Illusion** [00:11:06–00:13:39] [Slide 7]

  - **Data/details:** Two horizontal lines appear unequal; audience guesses "first" or "second," but both are **equal in length**.  
  - **Walkthrough:** Visual cues trick System 1 into seeing a difference that does not exist.  
  - **Takeaway:** System 1 always fires first and dominates perception; recognizing this prevents self-blame and builds awareness.

## BF-Lec01 – Stage 4: Portfolio-Level Thinking vs. Loss Fixation [00:13:39–00:18:17]

### a) What this stage answers

Why do retail investors fixate on 1–2 losing stocks inside a winning portfolio, and how does shifting to portfolio-level anchoring liberate long-term compounding?

### b) Detailed explanation

#### Retail Fixation on Individual Losers

Connecting psychology directly to portfolio management, the speaker shares his experience managing his **joint family's portfolio** of **16–17 stocks** funded by his father's hard-earned money [00:13:39–00:14:17]. Even when almost every position in the portfolio is profitable—for instance, 8 out of 10 stocks in green and 2 in red, or 16 profitable stocks with just **1 recently initiated position down \-3% to \-4%**—the family's very first question is invariably about the single losing stock [00:14:17–00:16:16]. Because of this relentless focus on temporary red lines, the speaker eventually hid the account password from them [00:15:37].

#### Shifting the Anchor to Portfolio-Level P\&L

Speaker's view: The single most liberating mental shift for a beginner or retail investor is moving their emotional anchor from **individual stock gains and losses** to **total portfolio-level gains and losses** [00:16:16–00:18:17]. Using a cricket analogy, having one star performer does not matter if the overall team loses—only the final portfolio outcome counts [00:16:16–00:17:43]. Over the remaining 6–8 weeks of the course, participants will learn concrete systems for buying and selling, but getting emotions and EQ right in this first class is the decisive foundation for holding multi-baggers [00:17:07–00:18:17].

### c) Examples & case studies

- **Example 1: Joint Family Portfolio (16–17 Stocks)** [00:13:39–00:16:16]  
    
  - **Context:** Real-life illustration of loss fixation inside a predominantly profitable portfolio.  
  - **Data/details:** **16–17 stocks** total (walkthrough also uses 10 stocks: 8 winners, 2 losers); almost all positions in profit except **1 newly built position at \-3% to \-4%**.  
  - **Walkthrough:** Whenever the portfolio is reviewed, attention immediately locks onto the \-3% to \-4% position rather than the overall portfolio return.  
  - **Takeaway:** Human psychology disproportionately weights small losses over broad gains; judge performance at the portfolio level, not line-by-line.


- **Example 2: India vs. Pakistan Cricket Match (Virat Kohli 100\)** [00:16:16–00:17:43]  
    
  - **Context:** Showing why an individual multi-bagger cannot compensate for a failing overall portfolio.  
  - **Data/details:** Pakistan scores **350**; Virat Kohli scores **100 runs**, but India is bowled out for **330** and loses the match.  
  - **Walkthrough:** Even with a "multi-bagger" century from Virat Kohli, the team as a whole lost because the aggregate score fell short.  
  - **Takeaway:** Anchor to total portfolio returns rather than celebrating one isolated multi-bagger or agonizing over one small loser.

## BF-Lec01 – Stage 5: Evolutionary Origins of System 1 & Objective Systems  [00:18:17–00:19:48]

### a) What this stage answers

Why does System 1 dominate human reactions in the stock market, and how can investors neutralize it?

### b) Detailed explanation

System 1 is not a personal flaw—it was hardwired into the human brain by evolution [00:18:17–00:19:13]. Four to five thousand years ago, when humans lived in the wild savanna, reacting instantly to potential danger was essential for survival. If a bush started shaking while walking through the jungle, pausing to analyze what was inside risked becoming a lion's or tiger's meal; running immediately was the winning strategy [00:18:17–00:19:13].

In the modern world, however, the environment has changed drastically, and that same fast, automatic wiring misfires [00:19:13–00:19:48]. In stock markets, System 1 is sometimes helpful, but often misleading. Speaker's view: the real solution is to strip System 1 out of investment decisions and replace it with an objective, rule-based system for buying and selling [00:19:48].

### c) Examples & case studies

- **Example: Jungle bush-shaking hypothetical**  [00:18:17–00:19:13] [Slide not available]  
    
  - **Context**: Explaining the evolutionary origin of System 1's speed.  
  - **Data/details**: Walking in a jungle 4,000–5,000 years ago when a bush suddenly shakes.  
  - **Walkthrough**: Two choices exist—run immediately (System 1\) or stand and investigate (System 2). In the wild, running immediately prevented being eaten by a tiger or lion.  
  - **Takeaway**: Survival-era speed became our default brain setting, even though modern financial markets punish knee-jerk reactions.


- **Example: Woman vs. floor cleaner visual illusion**  [00:19:13–00:19:48] [Slide: image not legible — content taken from transcript]  
    
  - **Context**: Illustrating how System 1 misinterprets visual cues in modern contexts.  
  - **Data/details**: An image on the slide appears at first glance to show a woman, but on closer inspection is actually a cleaner sweeping the floor.  
  - **Walkthrough**: Quick glances produce false conclusions; only slowing down reveals what is actually happening.  
  - **Takeaway**: Market events frequently look alarming at first glance, requiring objective verification before acting.

## BF-Lec01 – Stage 7: System 2 Crash Framework & Long-Term Base Rates  [00:24:48–00:26:52]  [TVGP: Valuation]

### a) What this stage answers

How should System 2 respond during a market crash, and what long-term base rate should anchor an investor's mindset?

### b) Detailed explanation

When the phrase "market crash" comes up, System 1 immediately triggers fear and urges selling [00:24:48–00:25:49]. To counter this, investors must activate System 2 and run an objective three-part check:

1. **Valuations**: How are the valuations of my stocks? [00:25:49] [TVGP: Valuation]  
2. **Business quality**: How is the underlying business quality of my portfolio? [00:25:49]  
3. **Objective exit rules**: Has my exit system—such as the **30-weekly moving average** or **V-Stop**—actually been triggered? [00:25:49–00:26:23]
