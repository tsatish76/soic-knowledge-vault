## BF-Lec02 – Stage 1: Media Narratives & System 1 Macro Fears  \[00:00:00–00:05:20\]

### a) What this stage answers

Why do dramatic macro headlines and media narratives trigger System 1 panic, and how quickly do those narratives flip?

### b) Detailed explanation

#### Macro Fear Headlines & System 1 Triggers

Over a 6–12 month window, investors are bombarded with existential market fears: the Adani-Hindenburg episode ("Adani will take over the market" / "banks will die" / "Bank of India will die"), the Russia-Ukraine war ("World War 3, nuclear weapons, Armageddon"), persistent inflation over three years, the US banking crisis following Silicon Valley Bank's bankruptcy, H1B visa ban fears ("Indian IT sector is dead"), and election-outcome catastrophizing (such as "if BJP loses the 2024 election, markets will collapse") \[00:00:00–00:02:35, 00:03:46–00:04:34\]. The speaker explicitly notes he has no political party bias—these examples illustrate how System 1 immediately jumps to extreme conclusions \[00:01:48–00:02:35, 00:04:34\].

#### Why Media Narratives Flip

Media outlets survive on covering drama; no newspaper has ever printed *"today everything was right, we don't have any news today"* \[00:03:46\]. Because news follows price action rather than fundamental reality, narratives reverse overnight once markets recover \[00:02:35–00:03:46\]. Speaker's view: Behavioural Finance is taught before fundamentals and technicals (which start in Classes 2 and 3\) because if your thinking and emotional quotient (EQ) are weak, you will always lose against yourself regardless of how much technical or fundamental knowledge you acquire \[00:04:34–00:05:20\].

### c) Examples & case studies

- **Example 1: 2016–2017 US Election (Donald Trump & S\&P 500\)**  \[00:02:35–00:03:46\] \[Slide not available\]  
    
  - **Context**: Demonstrating how media narratives reverse immediately to rationalize market price action.  
  - **Data/details**: Pre-election narrative claimed Donald Trump's win would bring anti-localization/trade barriers and hurt the S\&P 500\. On the day of victory, the S\&P 500 fell and then recovered the *very same day*.  
  - **Walkthrough**: Once the S\&P 500 rebounded intraday, media commentary immediately flipped to calling Trump "pro-capitalism, pro-business" and expecting pro-business policies.  
  - **Takeaway**: Media narratives are backward-looking stories designed to sell news, not reliable guides for buy/sell decisions.


- **Example 2: Recurring Indian & Global Macro Scares**  \[00:00:00–00:02:35, 00:03:46–00:04:34\] \[Slide not available\]

## BF-Lec02 – Stage 2: Market Reality vs. The "Perfect Tree" Illusion  \[00:05:20–00:11:16\]

### a) What this stage answers

Why do newer investors expect smooth, linear portfolio gains, and what does a real 10–15 year investing journey actually look like?

### b) Detailed explanation

#### Bull-Market Recency vs. Real Obstacles

Investors who entered the market recently—especially those trading SMEs or microcaps since 2017—often assume markets move upward in a straight line \[00:05:20–00:05:53\]. In reality, an investing journey resembles an obstacle course: you start, hit a trough, cross a wooden bridge, encounter deep water requiring a boat, and face a monsoon right before the finish line \[00:05:53–00:06:49\].

#### The "Perfect Tree" Visualization Experiment

The speaker asks participants to close their eyes and visualize a tree on a sunny day \[00:06:49–00:07:36\]. Almost everyone imagines a tall, green, symmetrical "perfect" tree \[00:07:36–00:08:23\]. Yet in nature, real trees take countless imperfect shapes—bent trunks, missing branches, uneven sunlight—while still growing \[00:08:23–00:09:10\]. Portfolios behave the exact same way: returns are **lumpy**, not smooth \[00:09:10–00:10:31\]. Experienced investors with 6–15 years in the market (such as Rajneesh in the comments) confirm that ups, downs, peaks, and drops are normal parts of compounding \[00:09:10–00:10:31\].

### c) Examples & case studies

- **Example 1: Sunny-Day Tree Visualization**  \[00:06:49–00:09:10\] \[Slide not available\]  
    
  - **Context**: Exposing the human mind's tendency to imagine idealized smoothness.  
  - **Data/details**: Mental image \= tall, green, symmetrical tree vs. real-world trees \= bent, asymmetric, uneven branches.  
  - **Walkthrough**: Just as the mind defaults to an idealized tree, new investors picture their portfolio compounding in a straight upward line.  
  - **Takeaway**: Expect irregular, imperfect paths in real-world equity compounding.


- **Example 2: Veteran Investors (10–15 Years) vs. Post-2017 Bull-Market Entrants**  \[00:05:53, 00:09:10–00:10:31\]  
    
  - **Context**: Grounding newcomers in realistic return expectations.  
  - **Data/details**: Investors with 6–15 years of experience confirm returns are "lumpy" with repeated drops and peaks.  
  - **Walkthrough**: Bull markets mask volatility, leading newcomers to believe markets always go up.  
  - **Takeaway**: Accept lumpiness and drawdowns upfront so bull-market expectations do not shatter during corrections.

## BF-Lec02 – Stage 3: Approximations, Irrational Markets & "Physics Envy"  \[00:11:16–00:15:40\]

### a) What this stage answers

Why is exact precision impossible in stock valuation, and why do engineers struggle most with this concept?

### b) Detailed explanation

#### Range Estimation Over Exactness

The speaker asks the audience to estimate the weight of a **Boeing 747**, receiving guesses like 10–20 tons, 5–30 tons, 25 tons, and 120 tons \[00:11:16–00:12:52\]. Framing it as a broad approximation (`1 kg to 200,000 kgs`), the speaker points out that real-world judgment relies on reasonable ranges rather than pinpoint accuracy \[00:12:04–00:12:52\]. Similarly, if you see a person aged 25–26 and 5'8"–5'9" tall, you can reasonably estimate their weight at 85–90 kg without needing a scale \[00:14:25\].

#### The Engineer's Mindset & Irrational Markets

Addressing the many engineers in the course, the speaker highlights a common trap: engineers demand an exact, precise fair value for every stock \[00:12:52–00:13:38\]. In real life, **precision does not exist** in valuation \[00:13:38–00:14:25\]. Why? Because markets are driven by human beings and institutions, and no human is rational all day long—people cycle through anger, sadness, happiness, and exuberance \[00:15:09–00:15:40\]. Since mass-scale market participants are irrational, markets can test both exuberance and patience far beyond what precise models predict \[00:14:25–00:15:40\].

### c) Examples & case studies

- **Example 1: Boeing 747 Weight Approximation**  \[00:11:16–00:12:52\] \[Slide not available\]  
    
  - **Context**: Teaching range-based thinking over exact calculation.  
  - **Data/details**: Audience guesses ranged from 5–30 tons to 120 tons; speaker framed a wide bound of `1 kg to 200,000 kgs`.  
  - **Walkthrough**: Without exact specifications, useful decisions come from bounding the range rather than chasing false precision.  
  - **Takeaway**: Valuation only requires knowing whether a stock is roughly cheap or expensive.


- **Example 2: Estimating a Person's Weight from Height & Age**  \[00:14:25\]  
    
  - **Context**: Showing how rough heuristics work in practice.  
  - **Data/details**: Age `25–26`, height `5'8"–5'9"` → estimated weight `85–90 kg`.  
  - **Walkthrough**: You do not need an exact gram-level figure to make a sound judgment.  
  - **Takeaway**: Apply rough-range estimation to stock valuations rather than seeking exact rupee targets.

On a system level, investors only need to judge roughly whether a business is expensive or cheap—there will always be multiple ways to interpret it \[00:15:40–00:16:16\]. Because markets take extremes to even further extremes when tailwinds kick in, valuations can expand dramatically \[00:16:16–00:17:00, 00:19:11\].

Expecting exact, formulaic answers in markets is **"physics envy"** (a term contrasting precise physics with imperfect finance) \[00:19:11–00:19:52\]. The speaker asks every participant to formally write down and commit: **"I accept"** that **finance is an imperfect art—50%–60% art and only 40%–50% science** \[00:19:52–00:20:30\].

## BF-Lec02 – Stage 5: Exponential Doubling Trap (Lily Pad Experiment)  \[00:20:30–00:22:06\]

### a) What this stage answers

Why does System 1's linear intuition fail on exponential growth problems, and how does the lily pad puzzle expose that reflex?

### b) Detailed explanation

Before summarizing System 1 and System 2, the speaker tests the audience with a classic exponential-growth puzzle \[00:20:30–00:21:14\]. A patch of lily flowers doubles in size every single day and takes **48 days** to cover an entire lake. Participants are asked how many days it takes to cover **half the lake**, with options of **24 days**, **47 days**, and **50 days** \[00:20:30–00:21:14\].

Audience responses include 24 days, 49 days, 50 days, and 1 day \[00:21:14\]. (Note: the transcript contains an apparent STT distortion stating `"80-85% got the answer correct. It's been 24 days"` `[?]`, immediately followed by the speaker's explicit mathematical explanation.) Because the patch doubles every day and reaches 100% coverage on Day 48, working backward one day means the lake must be **50% (half) covered on Day 47** \[00:21:14–00:22:06\]. Choosing 24 days is a classic System 1 "jumping to conclusions" error—dividing 48 by 2 linearly instead of thinking exponentially \[00:21:14–00:22:06\].

### c) Examples & case studies

- **Example: Lily Flower Lake Doubling Puzzle**  \[00:20:30–00:22:06\] \[Slide not available\]  
  - **Context**: Demonstrating how System 1 substitutes linear division for exponential reasoning.  
  - **Data/details**: Patch doubles daily; full lake covered in **48 days**. Options: 24, 47, 50 days. Correct answer \= **47 days**.  
  - **Walkthrough**: If Day 47 \= 50% coverage, doubling on Day 48 yields 100% coverage. (By contrast, Day 50 would imply doubling past 100%.)  
  - **Takeaway**: Never accept the first linear shortcut (48 ÷ 2 \= 24\) on compounding or exponential problems; pause and check the math.

### d) Formulas, metrics & thresholds (if any)

None stated.

### e) Visual aid

```
Day 47: 50% of lake covered
   × 2 (doubles in 1 day)
Day 48: 100% of lake covered
(Linear System 1 trap: 48 ÷ 2 = Day 24 ❌)
```

### f) Speaker's cautions & common mistakes

- Jumping to linear conclusions (halving 48 days to 24 days) on exponential processes \[00:21:14–00:22:06\].

## BF-Lec02 – Stage 6: System 1 vs. System 2 (*Thinking, Fast and Slow*)  \[00:22:06–00:23:49\]

### a) What this stage answers

What are the defining characteristics of System 1 and System 2, and why must psychology precede stock-watchlist building?

### b) Detailed explanation

#### Contrasting System 1 and System 2

Drawing from the book ***Thinking, Fast and Slow***, the speaker lays out the formal distinction between the two modes of thought \[00:22:06–00:23:49\]:

- **System 1**: Operates **fast, unconsciously, and automatically** with little or no effort and no sense of voluntary control \[00:22:06, 00:23:22\]. It is genuinely useful for everyday habits—such as waking up and brushing your teeth without thinking—and for fight-or-flight survival responses \[00:22:06–00:22:44\]. However, System 1 is **error-prone**, yet most webinar participants (including the speaker) rely on it most of the time \[00:22:44\].  
- **System 2**: Operates **slowly, consciously, and effortfully**, allocating attention to demanding mental activities such as complex computation \[00:22:44, 00:23:49\]. Its operations are associated with subjective agency, choice, and concentration, making it far more reliable for finance and investing \[00:22:44–00:23:49\].

#### Why Watchlists Wait Until Psychology Is Fixed

Addressing learners who want stock names immediately, the speaker explains that teaching watchlist construction on Day 1 would prevent participants from focusing on the psychological foundation first \[00:23:22\].

### c) Examples & case studies

- **Example: Brushing Teeth Habit (System 1 Utility)**  \[00:22:06\] \[Slide not available\]  
  - **Context**: Showing that System 1 is not "bad" everywhere—it is essential for daily routines.  
  - **Data/details**: Waking up and brushing teeth without conscious deliberation.  
  - **Walkthrough**: Automating routine physical habits frees mental bandwidth, whereas investing requires deliberate System 2 attention.  
  - **Takeaway**: Use System 1 for daily habits, and reserve System 2 for financial decisions.

### d) Formulas, metrics & thresholds (if any)

None stated.

### e) Visual aid

| Dimension | System 1 | System 2 |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Speed & Effort** | Fast, automatic, little/no effort | Slow, deliberate, effortful |
| **Awareness** | Unconscious, no voluntary control | Conscious, agency, choice, concentration |
| **Best Use** | Daily habits (brushing teeth), fight-or-flight | Complex computation, investing decisions |
| **Reliability in Markets** | Error-prone | Far more reliable |

### f) Speaker's cautions & common mistakes

- Rushing straight to stock names and watchlists before mastering behavioural psychology \[00:23:22\].
