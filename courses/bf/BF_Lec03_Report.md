## BF-Lec03 – Stage 1: Super Deprival Reaction Syndrome & FOMO  [00:00:00–00:03:17]

### a) What this stage answers

What is Super Deprival Reaction Syndrome, and why does it manifest in retail investors primarily as FOMO (Fear of Missing Out)?

### b) Detailed explanation

#### Everyday Triggers of Super Deprival Reaction Syndrome

The speaker opens with three relatable behavioral examples [00:00:00–00:02:32]. First, when driving (for instance in Delhi) and approaching an intersection as the green light turns yellow—or even red—drivers speed up rather than slowing down [00:00:00–00:00:47]. Second, if a friendly dog has a bone in its mouth and you snatch that bone away, it immediately turns aggressive—barking, chasing, or biting [00:00:47–00:01:31]. Third, when the speaker took a Kit Kat away from his nephew because he had not finished his homework, the child started crying and hitting him [00:02:03–00:02:32].

All three reactions demonstrate **Super Deprival Reaction Syndrome**: when something is already in someone's possession or about to be lost, taking it away triggers an intense, irrational defensive reaction [00:01:31–00:02:32]. (Transcript also notes a garbled phrase `"one-sided 14 injection"` `[?]` at [00:01:31].)

#### FOMO as Retail Investing's Largest Deprival Trap

In retail investing, the single biggest manifestation of Super Deprival Reaction Syndrome is **FOMO (Fear of Missing Out)**—the fear that others are making easy money while you are being left behind [00:02:32–00:03:17].

  - **Walkthrough**: Instead of braking safely, drivers accelerate to avoid missing the green phase.  
  - **Takeaway**: Perceived deprivation of an opportunity overrides rational caution.


- **Example 2: Dog with a Bone & Nephew's Kit Kat**  [00:00:47–00:02:32] [Slide not available]  
    
  - **Context**: Showing how taking away a perceived reward triggers immediate hostility or distress.  
  - **Data/details**: Friendly dog barks/chases/attacks when bone is snatched; nephew cries and hits when Kit Kat is withheld until homework is done.  
  - **Walkthrough**: Even a loving animal or child reacts disproportionately when something desirable is removed.

## BF-Lec03 – Stage 5: Anchor Bias Traps — Share Price, Taxes, NFOs & Shrinkflation  [00:14:35–00:19:00]

### a) What this stage answers

How does Anchor Bias distort decisions across stock prices, capital-gains taxes, mutual fund NFOs, and everyday consumer goods?

### b) Detailed explanation

#### The "Cheap Share Price" & NFO NAV Illusions

In retail investing, beginners frequently ask for "cheap shares under ₹100" [00:14:35]. Even major YouTube channels—who claim to teach finance—peddle videos titled *"3 stocks below ₹100"*, *"5 stocks below ₹200"*, or *"stocks below ₹50"* because they attract maximum views [00:14:35–00:15:55]. Both creators and viewers are exploiting **Anchor Bias**: the human mind latches onto an arbitrary reference number, distorting decisions in salary negotiations, medical diagnoses, purchases, and investing [00:15:55].

The exact same bias drives mutual fund **NFO (New Fund Offer)** marketing [00:17:17–00:17:31]. At least 3–4 fund houses market NFOs at a **₹10 NAV**, implying that a ₹10 NAV is "cheap" and will therefore perform better—a pure anchoring gimmick [00:17:17–00:17:31].

#### Tax Anchoring & Consumer Shrinkflation

Investors also anchor to **taxes**, refusing to sell an overvalued stock simply to avoid paying short-term capital gains tax [00:16:31]. Finally, consumer brands exploit anchoring through **shrinkflation**: over the last 10 years, with **6%–7% CAGR inflation**, the contents of a ₹10 Lay's packet have shrunk so much that the old quantity now costs ₹20—yet Lay's keeps the ₹10 price point because consumers are anchored to ₹10 [00:17:31–00:19:00].

### c) Examples & case studies

- **Example 1: YouTube "\<₹100 / \<₹200 Stock" Videos & ₹10 NFO NAVs**  [00:14:35–00:15:55, 00:17:17–00:17:31] [Slide not available]  
    
  - **Context**: Exposing price-per-share and NAV anchoring in retail finance.  
  - **Data/details**: Videos promoting stocks under ₹50/₹100/₹200 get maximum views; 3–4 fund houses market NFOs at ₹10 NAV.  
  - **Walkthrough**: Investors equate a low nominal share price or ₹10 NAV with cheapness or higher upside, ignoring market cap, earnings, and valuation.  
  - **Takeaway**: Nominal price per share or NFO NAV is meaningless; never anchor to arbitrary rupee tags.


- **Example 2: Ramdeo Agarwal & Pentamedia ("Pento media") Tax Anchor**  [00:15:55–00:17:17] [Slide not available]  
    
  - **Context**: Showing how anchoring to tax savings can trap even seasoned investors.  
  - **Data/details**: Ramdeo Agarwal ("Mr. Ram Deogarwal" in STT) held Pentamedia after a massive rally (`"went up a lot in the year 2020-30"` [? verify — likely 20–30x or 2000s cycle]).  
  - **Walkthrough**: He delayed selling because he did not want to pay short-term taxes and wanted to qualify for long-term capital gains—anchoring to tax avoidance over exit discipline.  
  - **Takeaway**: Never let tax anchoring override a sound sell decision.


- **Example 3: Lay's ₹10 Packet (Shrinkflation)**  [00:17:31–00:19:00] [Slide not available]  
    
  - **Context**: Everyday illustration of how companies work around consumer price anchoring.  
  - **Data/details**: **6%–7% CAGR** inflation over **10 years**; old ₹10 quantity now sells in a ₹20 packet while the ₹10 packet contains reduced volume.  
  - **Walkthrough**: Knowing buyers are anchored to ₹10, Lay's shrinks product quantity rather than raising the ₹10 sticker price.  
  - **Takeaway**: Anchoring blinds people to underlying value changes—whether in snack packets or stocks.

## BF-Lec03 – Stage 6: Positive Anchoring, Emotional Attachment & Endowment Bias  [00:19:00–00:22:44]  [TVGP: Valuation]

### a) What this stage answers

How can investors flip Anchor Bias into a positive edge, and why must they avoid emotional attachment and Endowment Bias?

### b) Detailed explanation

#### Re-Anchoring to Process Instead of Price (*Bhaav*)

While Anchor Bias can be destructive, investors can turn it into an advantage to strengthen EQ [00:19:00–00:19:45]. Instead of anchoring to **unrealized peak profits** (for example, fixating on a ₹15 lakh paper profit that shrinks during a market correction) or raw share price (**bhaav**) without context, investors should anchor to:

- **Exit systems** and alert systems [00:19:00, 00:19:45]  
- **Buying criteria** and personal investing style [00:19:00]  
- **Sector tailwinds**, **valuations**, and **price + volume action together** [00:19:00–00:20:23]

#### Overcoming Endowment Bias: "Part-Time Business Owners"

Investors frequently form emotional attachments to companies like IRCTC, Reliance, Sequent (`"Sequence"` in STT `[?]`), Adani, or HUL [00:20:23–00:21:08]. Speaker's view: *"You can give love to your family, but a stock won't love you back"* [00:21:08]. Even the greatest investors—Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger—make mistakes, and they cut them rather than clinging emotionally [00:21:08–00:22:13]. Because public-market investors are **"part-time business owners"**, we can ride a business for 10 years if no exit is triggered—or exit after 2 years if an exit rule triggers, without falling prey to **Endowment Bias** [00:22:13–00:22:44].

### c) Examples & case studies

- **Example 1: Unrealized Profit Anchor (₹15 Lakh) & Stock Attachment**  [00:19:45–00:21:08] [Slide not available]  
    
  - **Context**: Illustrating harmful emotional anchors in portfolio management.  
  - **Data/details**: Anchoring to a ₹15 lakh unrealized profit during a market correction; emotional attachment to IRCTC, Reliance, Sequent [?], Adani, HUL.  
  - **Walkthrough**: Fixating on past peak MTM or loving a company name prevents objective exits when price-volume or valuation context changes.  
  - **Takeaway**: Anchor to your system, valuations, and price-volume action—never to *bhaav* alone or emotional loyalty.


- **Example 2: Warren Buffett — Dexter Shoes, Chevron & Airlines**  [00:21:08–00:22:13] [Slide not available]  
    
  - **Context**: Proving that even legendary investors make mistakes and must sell without attachment.  
  - **Data/details**: Buffett bought **Dexter Shoes** via a share swap using Berkshire Hathaway shares, resulting in an estimated **$6–8 billion** opportunity-cost loss when Dexter Shoes failed; he also sold **Chevron** and **Airlines** at a loss (even though airlines later bounced back).  
  - **Walkthrough**: Had Buffett remained emotionally anchored to his purchase or thesis, he would not have exited Chevron or airlines.  
  - **Takeaway**: Public markets give us the advantage of being part-time owners—take exits objectively when triggered.

## 7\. GLOSSARY

- **Super Deprival Reaction Syndrome**: Psychological reaction where taking away (or threatening to take away) something desirable triggers disproportionate aggression or urgency—manifesting in markets as FOMO [00:01:31–00:03:17].  
- **FOMO (Fear of Missing Out)**: Anxiety that others are profiting while you are left behind, driving impulsive buying at peaks or in F\&O [00:02:32–00:03:17, 00:09:57].  
- **200 EMA / 200 DMA**: 200-day Exponential / Daily Moving Average used on TradingView to measure extreme price stretch (\>70%–80%) [00:03:17–00:05:12].  
- **Eiffel Tower Pattern**: Steep, parabolic chart spike (\>80% above 200 EMA) followed by sharp mean reversion; used descriptively [00:05:12].  
- **Reverse DCF**: Valuation method extracting implied market expectations from current stock price; used without full formula [00:05:12].  
- **Anchor Bias**: Cognitive bias where the mind latches onto an arbitrary reference number (e.g., \<₹100 share price, ₹10 NFO NAV, ₹10 Lay's packet, LTCG tax threshold, or ₹15L unrealized profit) [00:15:55–00:19:45].  
- **Shrinkflation**: Reducing product quantity while keeping the nominal sticker price fixed (e.g., Lay's ₹10 packet over 10 years of 6%–7% inflation) [00:18:15].  
- **Bhaav**: Hindi/Indian market term for raw stock price [00:20:23].  
- **Endowment Bias**: Overvaluing or refusing to sell an asset simply because you own it or feel emotionally attached to it [00:20:23–00:22:44].  
- **Part-Time Business Owners**: Speaker's framing of public-market equity investors who can hold for 10 years or exit cleanly in 2 years when exit rules trigger [00:22:13].  
- **Incentive-Caused Bias**: Tendency of people (auditors, managers, promoters) to act according to how they are paid or incentivized [00:22:44–00:23:41].  
- **Skin in the Game**: Substantial personal equity ownership (e.g., 80%–100% promoter stake or pre-tailwind warrants) aligning promoter wealth with business outcomes [00:24:30, 00:25:32–00:27:34].  
- **Chalta Hai Attitude**: Hindi idiom for a casual, complacent, "anything goes" management mindset when key executives lack ESOPs [00:24:58].
